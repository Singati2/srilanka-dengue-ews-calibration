Geospatial & Remotely-Sensed Risk Factors for Dengue Transmission

A citation-backed literature review to guide extension of a dengue early-warning exposure layer beyond temperature, humidity and precipitation — for areal health-unit × epidemiological-week modelling in Sri Lanka (RDHS divisions) and Colombia (municipalities).

Calibration & net-benefit early-warning study ERA5-Land + CHIRPS baseline Focus: additional geomatics-derivable drivers 59 peer-reviewed sources

This review catalogues every geomatics / remotely-sensed / geospatially-derivable factor with published evidence as a driver or effect-modifier of dengue, so the exposure layer extension misses nothing important. For each factor it records the direction and strength of association, the mechanism through the Aedes life cycle or human–vector contact, the operating spatial/temporal scale and lag, an open data product for derivation, and the factor's modelling role (driver / effect-modifier / confounder). Evidence from South Asia, Sri Lanka and tropical monsoon settings is flagged throughout, alongside conflicts and untested gaps.

2 · The top variables you cannot afford to miss

Beyond the ERA5-Land / CHIRPS temperature–humidity–precipitation core, these are the additions most strongly justified by the evidence and most readily derived from open geospatial products.

1. Elevation (and its interaction with temperature)

Aedes aegypti and Ae. albopictus historically thin out above ~1,500–2,000 m because cooler air lengthens the extrinsic incubation period and shortens vector survival; below ~1% of historical dengue cases in the Americas occur above 2,000 m [11]. But the ceiling is rising — established populations now reach 1,700–2,300 m in Mexico [10], 2,100 m in Nepal [12], and even ~2,900 m in Bolivia. In the Philippines, elevation shapes force-of-infection and interacts with population density: density drives transmission below ~1,200 m but not above [13]. For Sri Lanka's RDHS divisions and Colombia's municipalities — both spanning coast-to-highland gradients — elevation from a 30 m DEM is a near-free, high-value stratifier of the thermal envelope.

2. Built-up density / impervious surface / urbanisation

The single most consistently positive landscape predictor. Built-up area carries the highest information value among land-cover classes [24], is the dominant driver of intra-city dengue patterns in Hangzhou [26] and Bhopal [22], and correlates strongly with hotspots (ρ≈0.82) in Tamil Nadu [25]. Mechanistically, dense settlement concentrates hosts and Ae. aegypti breeding containers and raises local temperature. Derive from GHSL built-up, ESA WorldCover, or NDBI.

3. Diurnal temperature range (DTR)

A mechanistic must-have that mean temperature alone cannot capture. Large daily swings reduce midgut infection and vector survival; at warm mean temperatures (>18 °C, i.e. most of lowland Sri Lanka and Colombia) a larger DTR lowers transmission, while at cooler means it raises it [27][28][29]. Critically, this has been demonstrated locally: in Colombo, DTR>7.5 °C was negatively correlated with dengue at an 8-week lag [31]. Compute from ERA5-Land hourly temperature or MODIS day–night LST.

4. Land-surface temperature & urban heat islands

LST resolves within-city thermal heterogeneity that air-temperature reanalysis smooths over. Heat-island cores align with dengue hotspots, though the very hottest cores (>48 °C surface) can suppress vector survival — the relationship is unimodal around the ~29 °C optimum [25][30][57]. MODIS MOD11 (1 km) or Landsat TIRS (100 m) provide it cheaply.

5. Human mobility & connectivity

Movement, not mosquito flight, drives spatial spread and inter-unit synchrony. Mobile-phone-derived mobility predicted the geographic spread and timing of Pakistan's 2013 epidemic [35]; adding mobility improved provincial forecasts in Thailand [36]; and connectivity governs when environment-limited "patchy" emergence gives way to contiguous spread [37]. In Sri Lanka, the wet-zone epidemic synchrony is attributed to mobility in and out of Colombo [49]. Approximate with OSM road density, gravity/radiation models over WorldPop, or Meta Data-for-Good flows.

6. Population density

A robust positive driver and essential offset/confounder, interacting with elevation [13] and appearing in the best-fitting Sri Lankan GIS risk models [47]. WorldPop (100 m) or GHSL-POP make it trivial to include.

7. The drought→rain sequence & extreme rainfall

Beyond mean precipitation, the sequence matters. In 558 Brazilian microregions, extremely wet conditions raised risk at 0–3 months while drought raised it at 3–5 months — the latter concentrated in urban areas with intermittent water supply, because households store water [14]. A flooding scoping review of 131 studies found a short post-flood dip then a 1–4 month rise [15]. Capture with CHIRPS/GPM plus an SPI/SPEI drought index.

8. Household water storage & piped-water reliability

Among the strongest field-level determinants, though only indirectly remote-sensed. Lack of reliable tap water was a far stronger dengue risk factor than poverty per se in Delhi (adjusted OR 4.7) [38]; water-storage containers held 92–97% of Ae. aegypti immatures in rural Vietnam [40]; and in-house piped water was protective in Vientiane [46]. Proxy with DHS/JMP water-access surfaces and couple to the drought layer.

9. ENSO / large-scale climate oscillations

Provides multi-month lead time that local weather cannot. El Niño (ONI) raised dengue risk in Peru [32] and, directly relevant here, raised Aedes vector indices in Kalutara, Sri Lanka at a 6-month lag [33]; the Indian Ocean Dipole can suppress risk [58]. Free monthly indices (NOAA ONI, DMI).

10. Vegetation greenness — include, but as an effect-modifier

NDVI/EVI is the most conflicted variable and must be handled carefully. It is negative in dense-urban Colombo and across Sri Lankan districts [52][19], yet positive at 1-month lag in Cirebon (IRR 3.07) [23], and its sign flips with socioeconomic vulnerability in Brazil [20]. Treat it as a modifier/confounder — interacted with urbanisation and SES — not a standalone driver.

3 · Likely interactions & confounding with the existing T / RH / precipitation set

Bottom line: most of the new candidate variables are correlated with — or effect-modifiers of — the existing temperature, humidity and precipitation set. Adding them naïvely risks collinearity and double-counting; the value is in interactions and in resolving spatial heterogeneity the climate variables miss.

Elevation ↔ temperature

Elevation is largely a proxy for air temperature and the two are strongly collinear; elevation is best used to define suitability bounds or interacted with density rather than entered as an independent linear term [11][13].

DTR & LST ↔ mean temperature

DTR is orthogonal information the mean misses, but its sign depends on the mean — the interaction term (mean × range) is what carries the mechanism [27][28]. LST is correlated with air temperature but adds sub-unit spatial texture and heat-island structure [25][57].

Drought / water storage ↔ precipitation

The drought effect is a non-monotonic, lagged transform of precipitation, opposite in sign to the wet-season effect, and is mediated by water-supply reliability — so a simple linear rainfall term will mask it. Model rainfall non-linearly (DLNM) and add a separate drought index and a supply-shortage/storage proxy [14][40].

Absolute humidity ↔ relative humidity

Absolute humidity (from ERA5 dewpoint) captures the moisture load driving survival better than RH alone and showed strong lagged effects in India [22]; the two are correlated, so choose one or orthogonalise.

Urbanisation cluster (built-up · population · nighttime lights · SES · roads)

These co-vary tightly — nighttime-light brightness itself scales with population, GDP and road density [44]. They should be treated as one latent "urbanisation/exposure" construct (e.g. via a composite index or careful selection) to avoid collinearity, while retaining SES as a confounder for care-seeking and reporting.

Mobility ↔ everything spatial

Mobility interacts with both climate suitability and population, converting local weather-driven suitability into realised spread and cross-unit correlation [37][49]. In an areal week model it is best encoded through the neighbourhood/adjacency structure (gravity/radiation weights) rather than as a single covariate.

Healthcare access & reporting

Distance to care and surveillance intensity confound observed incidence independently of true transmission and should be adjusted for, especially when comparing rural RDHS divisions with Colombo, or remote Colombian municipalities with cities [17][56].

4 · Review / synthesis papers to read in full

Six synthesis papers give the most complete map of the field; the first four are the priority reads.

  1. Louis et al. 2014 — Modeling tools for dengue risk mapping: a systematic review (Int. J. Health Geographics). The foundational systematic review of predictors and spatial modelling approaches; explicitly flags entomological predictors, human mobility and serology as persistent gaps [1].
  2. Marti et al. 2020 — A Mapping Review on Urban Landscape Factors of Dengue from Earth Observation, GIS and Surveys (Remote Sensing). 78 articles; builds a co-occurrence network of land-cover, land-use and housing factors with signed associations — the best single guide to landscape covariates [2].
  3. Yin et al. 2022 — Systematic Review on Modeling Methods and Influential Factors for Mapping Dengue Risk in Urban Settings (IJERPH). Focuses on urban-scale methods and factor selection; argues socioeconomic and connectivity data best capture within-city heterogeneity [3].
  4. Chaiyarit et al. 2025 — Environmental health factors influencing dengue: a systematic review with thematic categorization (Int. J. Environ. Health Res.). 64 studies distilled into eight environmental-health themes (water storage, waste, housing, drainage, vegetation, urbanisation, climate, water-supply reliability) — directly maps onto exposure-layer design [9].
  5. Li et al. 2022 — Big Geospatial Data and Data-Driven Methods for Urban Dengue Risk Forecasting: A Review (Remote Sensing). Catalogues predictors, open data sources, scales and modelling methods across 53 recent studies — useful for data-product choices [5].
  6. Gao et al. 2021 — Land use / land cover change and dengue in China: a systematic review (PLoS NTD). Detailed treatment of how LULC change, green space and blue space act on dengue, including where the evidence conflicts [6].

Also worth scanning: Pakaya et al. 2023 scoping review of DHF spatial models [7]; Liu et al. 2025 systematic review of geospatial epidemiology for vector-borne diseases [8]; Coalson et al. 2021 scoping review on flooding and mosquito-borne disease [15]; Mulligan et al. 2015 on the contested dengue–poverty link [39].

5 · Strong globally but under-tested in South Asia / Sri Lanka — potential contributions

Each of these is a defensible novelty claim for the study: a factor with solid evidence elsewhere but little or no formal test in Sri Lankan RDHS divisions.

Drought→rain sequence & intermittent water supply

The delayed drought effect is well established in Brazil [14] and mechanistically tied to household storage, which dominates Aedes production in South Asia [40][38] — yet Sri Lankan modelling remains centred on rainfall/temperature/humidity and rarely encodes a drought index or supply-reliability layer [47][48][51]. A DLNM drought term for RDHS divisions would be genuinely new.

Diurnal temperature range at areal scale

DTR is only tested for Colombo district (a single time series) [31]; a systematic DTR × mean-temperature interaction across all RDHS divisions and Colombian municipalities has not been done and is mechanistically well-motivated [27][28].

Landscape fragmentation / greenspace morphology

Edge density and greenspace aggregation have measurable, opposing effects in China [21] but have not been quantified for South Asian monsoon settings, where NDVI evidence is contradictory [19][23].

Nighttime lights & artificial light at night

VIIRS nighttime lights as an urbanisation/SES proxy — and ALAN as a driver of extended Aedes biting [43] — are essentially untested in Sri Lankan dengue models despite being trivially derivable at 500 m.

Formal human-mobility connectivity structure

Mobility is invoked qualitatively to explain wet-zone synchrony in Sri Lanka [49] but has not been built into an areal-week model there via gravity/radiation weights, as done for Thailand and Pakistan [35][36].

Topographic Wetness Index / HAND flood-proneness

DEM-derived wetness and height-above-nearest-drainage indices are standard in hydrology and plausibly capture stagnant-water pooling, but are rarely used as dengue covariates anywhere, let alone in Sri Lanka [16][17].

Note on the two study regions. Sri Lankan evidence is strongest for rainfall (positive, ~2–10 wk lag), the wet-zone vs dry-zone contrast in periodicity, population density, and ENSO at long lead [47][49][33][51]; temperature associations are inconsistent across studies [47][51][52]. Colombian evidence is dominated by vector-distribution/altitude work showing Aedes now reaching >2,500 m and both species co-occurring across elevation and urban–rural gradients [5*] — implying elevation, urbanisation and species composition are the key modifiers to encode for municipalities. (*Colombian vector-ecology sources reviewed but not all individually numbered here; see Mantilla-Granados 2025 and Echeverría/Mejía-Jurado niche models for municipality-level altitude ranges.)
