Understanding the Dengue Early-Warning Calibration Study

A simple, section-by-section tour of the whole research project — what the big question is, what every folder and file does, what was found, and what still needs doing.

Repository: srilanka-dengue-ews-calibration · Written for a collaborator opening this folder for the first time.

The 60-second version

Dengue is a mosquito-borne disease. Health officials want an early-warning system that tells them "an outbreak is likely in a few weeks — get ready." Many researchers build these using weather data (rain and temperature drive mosquitoes). This study asks a sharper, more practical question:

When two warning systems look equally accurate on paper, do they actually lead to different real-world decisions? And — surprisingly — it finds that just watching recent case counts is usually a stronger, cheaper warning signal than weather models. Weather only adds a little, and only when combined with recent cases. The study's real contribution is the rigorous way it measures usefulness, borrowed from medical research, which the dengue field rarely uses.

1What is this study about?

Imagine you run public health in a region. Every week you must decide: do I raise a dengue alert? Raising an alert costs money and effort (spraying, clinic prep, public messaging). Not raising one when an outbreak comes is far worse. So you want a warning system that helps you make the right call more often.

Researchers have built many dengue warning systems. Most are judged on a score called AUC — roughly, "how well can the model tell outbreak weeks apart from calm weeks?" But the researchers here noticed a gap: a good AUC score does not automatically mean the model helps you make better decisions. Two models can have the same AUC and yet, in practice, one triggers smarter alerts than the other.

The core question

When two climate-driven dengue warning systems have comparable accuracy (AUC), do they lead to different public-health alert decisions once you properly measure them with calibration and net-benefit analysis?

Importantly, the team did not invent a new forecasting model. They took existing, published model types and put them through a much more honest, decision-focused evaluation than the field normally does. That evaluation — not a shiny new algorithm — is the contribution.

Analogy: Two weather apps both claim "90% accurate." But if one keeps you dry and the other makes you carry an umbrella on 200 sunny days, they are not equally useful. This study measures the umbrella-carrying, not just the accuracy badge.

2Key words, explained simply

You'll see these terms everywhere in the folder. Here they are in plain English.

TermWhat it means (plainly)
DengueA viral disease spread by mosquitoes. Outbreaks come in waves, partly driven by rain and heat.
Early-warning system (EWS)A model that predicts, weeks ahead, whether an outbreak is coming — so officials can prepare.
Outbreak labelThe yes/no answer we're trying to predict. Here: "were cases this week above that area's usual high level (its 75th-percentile)?"
Horizon (h)How far ahead we predict. h=4 means "will there be an outbreak 4 weeks from now?"
AUCAn accuracy score from 0.5 (useless coin-flip) to 1.0 (perfect). Measures how well the model ranks risky weeks above calm ones.
CalibrationDo the model's probabilities mean what they say? If it says "70% chance," does an outbreak really happen ~70% of the time? A model can rank well (good AUC) but still be badly calibrated.
RecalibrationA simple correction that fixes a model whose probabilities have drifted (e.g., cases are generally rising over time).
Net benefit / Decision-curve analysis (DCA / DNB)The star of the study. It turns a model into a single "is this worth acting on?" number by weighing the good of catching outbreaks against the cost of false alarms. This is standard in medicine but rare in dengue research.
Threshold (p*)The probability at which you decide to raise an alert. The study mostly uses p* = 0.30 (act if risk ≥ 30%).
DLNM"Distributed-lag non-linear model" — a well-known statistical way to capture that weather affects mosquitoes with a delay (rain today → mosquitoes in a few weeks).
Frozen datasetA dataset locked and fingerprinted (a SHA256 checksum) so it can never silently change. Guarantees everyone analyzes exactly the same numbers.
RDHSSri Lanka's health administrative regions ("Regional Director of Health Services" areas). There are 26 of them — the map units for the Sri Lanka data.

3The models being compared

The study lines up several models from simplest to most complex — a "ladder." Each rung adds information. The key contest is: can weather-based models beat simply watching recent case counts?

ModelWhat it uses to predictPlain description
M0Season onlyThe "dumb" baseline — just the time of year. A floor to beat.
M1Recent case countsWatches how many cases happened in recent weeks. Simple, surveillance-only.
M2Weather onlyRain + temperature (with delays). No case data.
M3Weather + season + placeM2 plus seasonal and location adjustments.
M4Recent cases + weatherThe hybrid: combines M1 and M2.
M5Hybrid + season + placeThe fullest model. Usually the best performer.

There is also a proper DLNM comparator (a more sophisticated weather model), built in both Python and the gold-standard R package, to make sure the weather models were given a fair, credible shot.

The three takeaways

1
Recent cases beat weather. Just watching case counts (M1) is a strong, hard-to-beat warning signal. Weather-only models (M2, M3) are close to useless for decisions — no better than "always alert."
2
Weather helps only as a hybrid, and only a little. Adding weather to recent cases (M5) gives a small but real improvement in Colombia (net-benefit gain +0.018, roughly ~1.8 better alert decisions per 100 area-weeks). In Sri Lanka the same gain was too small to confirm.
3
Calibration drifts, but is fixable. Over time the models' probabilities became "off" (because case rates rose). A simple recalibration corrects this without changing which model wins.
Honest caveat the team itself raises

A popular belief is "weather is most useful for long-range warnings." The study tested horizons from 1 to 12 weeks and did not find that pattern — weather's small edge stayed flat and even shrank further out. The team is careful not to over-claim.

5Two countries, one story

The study runs in two places to see if the findings hold up. Understanding this split explains why the folder has both "Sri Lanka" files and lots of "Colombia" files.

SettingRoleDataMap unitsResult
🇱🇰 Sri LankaPrimary studyHand-built from weekly government PDF reports (WER), 2018–202526 RDHS regionsRecent cases win; hybrid gain not confirmed
🇨🇴 ColombiaExternal check (replication)Public "OpenDengue" database, ~1,000 municipalities, 2006–2022MunicipalitiesSame story, but here the hybrid gain was confirmed (bigger dataset)

Colombia was added to test whether the Sri Lanka findings were a fluke. They largely held up — which strengthens the paper. (Sri Lanka data had to be built from scratch because no ready-made subnational dataset existed; Colombia's did, via OpenDengue.)

7The docs/ library — organized by theme

There are about 78 documents. Don't panic — they come in two flavors and five topics. A spec is a plan written before doing something ("here's what we'll do and why"). A report is written after ("here's what we found"). They usually come in pairs.

  • opendengue_*.md (several) — checking whether the OpenDengue database could serve Sri Lanka (it can't at region level — that's why the PDFs were needed).

Theme C — Colombia analysis (the biggest cluster)

Many files named colombia_*. Each has a spec + report pair:

  • colombia_label_* — defining what counts as an "outbreak."
  • colombia_lag_feature_assembly_* — building the "weather from N weeks ago" columns.
  • colombia_model_ladder_* — running M0→M5 and scoring them (the main results).
  • colombia_horizon_sensitivity_* — does the answer change at 1, 2, 8, 12 weeks ahead?

  • dlnm_climate_comparator_*, canonical_R_dlnm_* — giving the weather model its fairest, gold-standard shot.
  • hybrid_model_extension_*, rolling_recalibration_extension_* — the hybrid and the drift-fix.
  • biomodel_feasibility_memo_v1.md — a decision not to add a complex mechanistic mosquito model (it wouldn't be reliable here).

The 3-step pipeline

harvest → extract → QC. Download the PDFs, pull the numbers out, then check them. This runs entirely from public sources, so anyone can reproduce the dataset and verify it matches the frozen fingerprint.

9Data, quality-control & "freezing"

A cornerstone of this project is data discipline. The raw data never goes into the shared repository; instead the folder documents exactly what the data is and proves its quality.

The frozen Sri Lanka dataset

WhatWeekly dengue counts, 2018–2025
Size10,790 rows = 415 weekly reports × 26 regions
Real data cells10,757 valid; only 33 missing (documented); 0 invalid values
Main columndengue_current_week (the outcome). dengue_cumulative is QC-only, never analyzed.
Manual check33 reports re-typed by hand — 0 mismatches
FingerprintA SHA256 checksum locks it so it can never silently change

Read about this in qc_summaries/ (the FREEZE_LOG.md and QC reports) and data_dictionary/ (defines each column).

Why "freezing" matters

Once frozen, everyone on the team analyzes exactly the same numbers, and any accidental change is instantly detectable. This is what makes the research reproducible and trustworthy.

The golden rule (in README & .gitignore)

Never commit raw PDFs, the frozen data files, weather rasters, credentials, or private emails. The .gitignore blocks these automatically, and there's a "forbidden-file guard" to run before saving changes. If you're new: don't drag data files into this folder.

10Geomatics — maps, boundaries & population

To turn case counts into rates (cases per person) and to do spatial analysis, you need three things: accurate region boundaries, population numbers, and neighbor relationships between regions. This work-stream ("WP1") is essentially complete.

  • Boundaries: official Sri Lanka maps, with a tricky case solved — Ampara district is served by two health regions (Ampara + Kalmunai), so 25 districts become 26 regions. The split was resolved using the official health-office list.
  • Population: WorldPop satellite estimates, corrected to match the 2024 census (WorldPop was ~5.6% too high, unevenly across regions — exactly the kind of bias that would distort a spatial study).
  • Neighbors: a "who-borders-whom" graph (26 regions, 60 borders, no impossible across-sea links) for spatial modeling.
  • Weather: temperature/humidity (ERA5-Land) and rainfall (CHIRPS) assembled into a weekly table for 2018–2025.

The geomatics_templates/ folder holds the blank templates (CSV files) used to organize this work.

12Open problems the paper still needs to fix

The internal mock peer review was refreshingly honest. If you're joining now, these are the live issues — likely exactly what your senior wants help with.

1
The "registered p*=0.30" claim needs proof. The paper calls the 30% alert threshold "preregistered," but there's no public registration document (DOI) to point to — and the team's own analysis found the strongest results at other thresholds. This must be justified or the word "registered" removed.
2
Colombia's win overlaps the COVID years (2020–2022). The one confirmed weather-hybrid benefit sits in a pandemic-disrupted period that didn't get the same anomaly check Sri Lanka got. Needs a sensitivity test excluding it.
3
"Confirmed vs not confirmed" may be over-stated. Colombia's and Sri Lanka's hybrid gains are statistically similar; calling one a win and the other a null is a classic statistical trap. Better to report both with error bars.
4
Missing Data/Code Availability section & the Colombia data citation. For a paper whose selling point is a reusable method, the code and data sources must be formally listed.
5
Translate the numbers into plain units. "Net benefit +0.018" sounds tiny; "~1.8 better alert decisions per 100 area-weeks" is what it actually means.

The overall plan (see paper_strategy_reframe_memo.md) is to reframe the paper from "a negative result" into "a better way to evaluate dengue warnings, demonstrated in two countries" — a more positive, publishable story. Target journal: PLOS Neglected Tropical Diseases.

One sentence to remember

This project's real product is not a model — it's a rigorous, honest way to judge whether a dengue warning system is actually worth acting on, and the finding that recent case counts are a surprisingly strong baseline that weather models only slightly improve upon.
