M6 — The Geomatics-Only Model

Why each landscape variable might matter for dengue — in plain language.

What M6 is (and isn't). M6 is a curiosity / safety-check model built only from maps of the land — terrain, cities, vegetation, water — with no case counts and no weather data. It asks one honest question: can the shape of a place, on its own, warn us about dengue? The likely answer is “barely,” and that’s a useful finding, not a failure. M6 is a comparison model, never the headline. It is measured against the existing M0–M5 models on the exact same weeks, so the comparison is fair.
Static — barely changes over years (terrain, city footprint) Dynamic — changes week to week (heat, greenness)

Terrain & water — the shape of the ground

Where mosquitoes can live and breed is set partly by the land itself.

1 Elevation

Why it matters: Mosquitoes and the virus struggle in the cold air of high ground. Above roughly 1,500 m dengue mostly disappears — this single layer explains why Colombia’s Andes and highlands stay pale on our maps. The clearest, strongest spatial signal we have.

StaticDownload: trivialFree (Copernicus/SRTM)

2 Slope

Why it matters: Flat land lets water sit still in puddles and containers — perfect nurseries. Steep slopes drain fast and offer fewer breeding spots.

StaticDownload: trivialFrom the same elevation map

3 Topographic Wetness Index (TWI)

Why it matters: A calculation of where water naturally collects after rain. High-wetness pockets hold standing water longer — more breeding. Rarely tested in Sri Lanka, so it’s a possible original contribution.

StaticDownload: lowComputed from elevation

4 HAND — flood-proneness

Why it matters: Measures how far above the nearest stream a place sits — a clean way to spot flood-prone ground. Floods first wash breeding sites away, then leave pooled water and disrupt mosquito control, raising risk weeks later.

StaticDownload: lowComputed from elevation

11 Surface water & distance to water

Why it matters: Ponds, rivers and marsh edges can shelter mosquitoes — yet wide open water often does the opposite. Proximity to water bodies is a classic, if mixed, risk clue.

StaticDownload: easyFree (JRC Global Surface Water)

Cities & people — where dengue thrives

Dengue’s mosquito loves dense, built-up, human places.

5 Built-up / impervious surface

Why it matters: Aedes aegypti is a city mosquito. Concrete, roofs and dense housing mean more water containers and more people to bite — a strong, consistent driver.

Static-ishDownload: easyFree (GHSL / WorldCover)

6 Population density

Why it matters: More people packed together means the virus jumps between hosts more easily. Matters most in low-lying, warm areas where the mosquito already thrives.

Static-ishDownload: easyFree (WorldPop)

7 Nighttime lights

Why it matters: Satellite photos of city lights are a handy stand-in for wealth, roads, and how built-up and active a place is. Updated monthly, so it can also track change.

Dynamic (monthly)Download: easyFree (VIIRS)

8 Land-cover fragmentation

Why it matters: It’s not just how much green or city, but how it’s patterned. Broken-up patches with many edges create the mixed habitats where mosquitoes and people meet. An under-explored angle.

StaticDownload: easy + some computing

14 Socioeconomic status / wealth

Why it matters: Poorer areas may have less reliable water and more open storage (breeding sites) — but the pattern flips in some places, so it’s a context clue, not a rule.

StaticDownload: moderateFree (Meta Relative Wealth Index)

15 Healthcare access

Why it matters: Travel time to clinics mostly shapes whether cases get counted, not whether they happen. We include it to stay honest about reporting — it is a detection clue, not a true driver.

StaticDownload: moderateSurveillance confounder

13 Human mobility / connectivity

Why it matters: Dengue travels with people. Well-connected hubs seed outbreaks in the places they link to. We build this from population and roads (a “gravity” model) — no private phone data needed.

Living surface — heat & greenness that change weekly

These are the “alive,” week-to-week layers, treated with time-lags like the weather.

9 Land-surface temperature / urban heat

Why it matters: Warmth speeds up the mosquito’s life and the virus inside it — up to about 29 °C, after which extreme heat becomes harmful. Satellite heat maps reveal hot spots inside cities that coarse weather data misses.

Dynamic (8-day)Download: moderateFree (MODIS)

10 Vegetation greenness (NDVI / EVI)

Why it matters: Green cover can shelter mosquitoes — or, in dense cities, mark the leafier wealthy areas with fewer cases. The signal flips by setting, so we treat it as a modifier, not a simple up-or-down driver.

Dynamic (16-day)Download: moderateFree (MODIS)

12 Forest cover

Why it matters: Dense forest usually means fewer of the city-loving dengue mosquito — though plantations can favour a different, related species. A useful negative marker.

Static-ishDownload: easyFree (WorldCover / Hansen)

16 Rice paddy / cropland

Why it matters: Flooded paddies and marshy field edges create breeding water, especially in the countryside and city fringes. Its rhythm follows the growing season, so we track how strongly greenness cycles through the year.

SeasonalDownload: moderateFree (WorldCover + MODIS)

How we’ll judge M6 — fairly

The whole point is a clean, apples-to-apples comparison.

We do not re-run the existing models. M6 is scored on the exact same weeks and places as M0–M5, using the same scoring code and settings. Then we ask three plain questions:

• Does landscape alone beat just knowing the season (M0)?
• Does it match weather-only (M2)?
• Can it approach recent case counts (M1), the tough baseline to beat?

And critically — not just “is it accurate,” but does it change the right public-health decision (net benefit). A model can look sharper yet help no one. If M6 quietly adds nothing, that honest result supports this study’s central message: don’t overclaim.
