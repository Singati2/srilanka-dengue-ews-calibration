# Cover letter (draft — one page)

Dear Editors of PLOS Global Public Health,

We submit our manuscript, "Does climate information add decision value beyond dengue surveillance? A specification-matched evaluation in Sri Lanka and Colombia," for consideration as a **Research Article**.

Climate-informed dengue early-warning systems are widely promoted, yet they are typically judged by discrimination and against weak comparators such as seasonal climatology. Our study asks the operationally decisive question instead: does climate information improve calibrated, threshold-based alert decisions beyond the recent-case surveillance that agencies already hold? Using a retrospective, decision-analytic framework (calibration, time-updated recalibration, and decision-curve net benefit) in two deliberately different settings—Sri Lanka (district-week, 2018–2025) and Colombia (municipality-week, 2020–2022)—we find that recent surveillance is a strong benchmark, that climate-only models add little decision value, and that a specification-matched climate increment is small, larger for non-climate seasonal and geographic structure than for climate itself, and separated from zero in Sri Lanka only after time-respecting recalibration. We report these uncertain and partly null findings transparently, including that the raw Sri Lanka interval includes zero.

The contribution is methodological and operational, and we believe it fits the journal's remit for rigorous global-public-health research, including methods and null or uncertain findings. Its practical message—benchmark climate-enhanced models against strong surveillance, match non-climate structure before attributing value to climate, and judge decision value under realistic recalibration—directly informs how resource-constrained dengue-control programs in LMIC settings should appraise climate-health investments. The Sri Lanka and Colombia case studies provide cross-setting evidence across South Asia and Latin America while avoiding any claim of equal magnitude or transportability. The analysis is predictive and decision-analytic; we make no causal claims.
